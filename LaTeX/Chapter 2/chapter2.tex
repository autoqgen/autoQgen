\section{Introduction}

This chapter reviews the key literature on digital question banks, automated question generation, review workflows, test assembly, access control, and AI-enabled web application security. The goal is to situate AutoQgen within the broader landscape of assessment systems and to clarify how its components relate to established research and practical system design. Rather than treating these topics as isolated techniques, the review examines how they are connected in a complete question-management and paper-generation workflow.

\section{Digital Question Bank and Assessment Systems}

Digital assessment systems organize questions as reusable assessment items with metadata, answer information, and academic context. A question bank typically supports categorization, search, filtering, and reuse across assessments. Platforms such as Moodle demonstrate how question repositories can be integrated with course delivery and quiz creation, while standards such as QTI provide interoperable representations for assessment items and tests \cite{moodleQuestionBank,moodleQuestionCategories,imsQti3}. These systems show the importance of metadata such as topic, difficulty, item type, marks, and review status, which support both retrieval and controlled reuse.

The literature also distinguishes between representation and workflow. A repository may store valid item data without guaranteeing that the item has been reviewed, approved, or properly assigned to an academic context. Likewise, an interoperability format does not by itself provide the governance mechanisms required for a functioning assessment system. For this reason, digital question-bank research and test-assembly methods are complementary rather than redundant \cite{kurdi2020,vanDerLinden2005}. A complete workflow must coordinate item creation, review, availability, selection constraints, and output generation.

\subsection{Existing Systems and Their Limitations}

Existing systems and research approaches are best understood by their scope. Moodle offers a general question bank and quiz-oriented reuse model; QTI provides a specification for item and test interchange; automated question generation studies focus on producing candidate questions from text or structured inputs; and automatic test assembly research formalizes selection under test-design constraints. Each addresses part of the assessment lifecycle, but not necessarily the full workflow of authoring, reviewing, approving, reusing, and exporting papers.

Repository features support storage and reuse, standards support interchange, generation research addresses candidate creation, and test-assembly methods address selection constraints. A complete educational platform must coordinate these functions and preserve quality, governance, and organizational boundaries.

\section{Automated Question Generation}

Automated Question Generation (AQG) studies computational methods for producing questions from texts, structured knowledge, or learning contexts. In educational settings, generated questions may support practice, formative assessment, or the preparation of candidate test items. AQG is not a single method: systems differ in their input representation, question types, level of automation, controllability, and validation procedures \cite{kurdi2020}.

Research in this area spans rule-based, statistical, and neural approaches. Rule-based systems rely on templates or transformations; statistical approaches rank candidate questions according to learned quality signals; and neural models generate questions directly from source content or context \cite{mitkovHa2003,hoshinoNakagawa2005,heilmanSmith2010,du2017,kurdi2020}. These methods show that generation can be useful, but they also show that language generation alone does not guarantee curricular alignment, answer validity, or suitability for formal assessment.

\subsection{Rule-Based and AI-Assisted Approaches}

Rule-based methods can provide explicit control when the input structure is known. Mitkov and Ha describe a computer-aided multiple-choice test-generation system, while Hoshino and Nakagawa examine real-time multiple-choice generation for language testing. Heilman and Smith further show how ranking methods can improve question-generation quality by identifying better candidate questions from a wider pool \cite{mitkovHa2003,hoshinoNakagawa2005,heilmanSmith2010}. These systems are valuable because they use clear transformation rules and scoring procedures, although their coverage depends on the rules, templates, or data available to them.

Neural approaches extend this line of work by learning mappings from source passages or structures to question text. Du, Shao, and Cardie demonstrate early neural question generation in reading-comprehension settings, while later reviews show the broader growth of AQG research and its methodological diversity \cite{du2017,kurdi2020}. Even when such systems produce fluent output, the generated result still requires assessment of correctness, context, and instructional suitability before it can be treated as approved content.

\section{Role-Based Access Control in Educational Platforms}

Educational platforms involve several distinct responsibilities: authors create content, reviewers decide whether it is accepted, and administrators manage users, organizations, and operational settings. A system that applies a single undifferentiated permission model to all these tasks would not reflect the actual separation of duties required in practice.

Role-Based Access Control (RBAC) addresses this problem by assigning permissions to roles and then mapping users to those roles. The foundational RBAC model formalizes the relationship between roles, permissions, and authorization decisions, and it remains highly relevant for systems with multiple user responsibilities \cite{sandhu1996rbac}. In educational platforms, permissions may concern question creation, review, paper construction, taxonomy management, or administrative actions. Those roles must be enforced on the server side, not only hidden in the interface.

\subsection{Authentication and Authorization}

Authentication establishes the identity associated with a session, but authorization determines whether that identity may perform a specific operation on a given resource. This separation is central in secure web applications: a role name alone is not proof that a request has been checked correctly. Server-side authorization and resource-scoped checks are necessary to prevent users from acting beyond their permitted domain \cite{sandhu1996rbac,owaspAccessControl}.

This is important where ownership, review, and administrative tasks are distinct. A user who can edit one question should not necessarily be allowed to edit every question or manage all organization resources. Object-level authorization therefore remains essential in systems with multiple content lifecycles and roles.

\subsection{Organization-Level Access Control}

Organization-aware systems add another dimension to access control. A user may have a global account role, a separate role within a particular organization, and possibly team-level responsibilities. These scopes must not be conflated. The separation is especially important in multi-tenant educational systems where each organization manages its own context and content boundaries \cite{krebs2012}.

The relevant security principle is that authorization must check both the user identity and the organization scope relevant to the resource. This ensures that actions remain limited to the current organization and that data isolation is enforced at the application boundary rather than only by interface filtering \cite{owaspAccessControl,owaspApi2023}. AutoQgen follows this pattern through separate global and organization-level permission matrices, which reflect the broader RBAC and multi-tenant design problem in educational software.

\section{AI Integration and Web Application Security}

Integrating AI services into a web application introduces additional concerns around validation, trust boundaries, and operational reliability. Model-generated output should be treated as untrusted input until it has been parsed, normalized, and checked against application rules. This is particularly important when the output is later used to create or approve assessment content \cite{owaspLlm2025,nistAiRmf2023,nistGenAiProfile2024}.

These concerns intersect with standard web security practices. Authentication and authorization protect user actions; input validation restricts unsafe or malformed data; rate limiting reduces abuse; and credentials must remain protected on the server side. For AI-enabled systems, this is not enough: structured-output validation, error handling, and human review remain necessary because model output can be syntactically valid yet pedagogically or factually unsound.

\subsection{AI Service Integration and Output Validation}

AI services may return text in inconsistent or malformed formats, even when the prompt requests a structured response. A robust system therefore validates the output against an expected schema, normalizes accepted fields, and rejects or reports malformed data before persistence. Research on structured output and LLM reliability emphasizes that prompt design alone does not guarantee valid or faithful responses \cite{liu2024Structured,owaspLlm2025}. In educational settings, validity also requires instructional and domain checks, not just syntactic correctness.

AutoQgen follows this pattern by normalizing AI-generated candidates, checking for duplicates or structural issues, presenting them to the user, and importing them only after explicit selection. This approach combines automation with human oversight and is more defensible than treating model output as textbook-quality assessment content automatically.

\subsection{Web Application Security}

Web application security depends on several distinct controls. Authentication establishes identity, session management maintains it securely, and authorization checks each protected action and resource. Passwords should be stored using a suitable hashing mechanism, and application inputs should be validated server-side. Security guidance from OWASP and related standards highlight the persistent importance of these controls in application development and API design \cite{owaspAsvs,owaspTop10,owaspApi2023}.

For AI-enabled educational applications, additional attention is required to prompt-injection risks, unsafe downstream use of model output, and resource misuse. These risks do not replace ordinary web security, but they expand the system-level requirements. The relevant lesson is that security claims must be evaluated against the controls actually implemented, not simply cited from a framework or standard.

\section{Research Gaps and Positioning of AutoQgen}

The reviewed literature addresses several important components of assessment systems: question-bank platforms provide categorization and reuse; QTI supports item and test interchange; AQG research investigates candidate generation; semantic similarity methods compare textual representations; and test-assembly research formalizes item selection under constraints \cite{moodleQuestionBank,imsQti3,kurdi2020,reimers2019sbert,vanDerLinden2005}. Each contribution has a specific scope, and the system-level challenge is to integrate these capabilities while preserving question status, academic context, reviewer decisions, and assessment constraints.

Several integration issues follow from this comparison. First, generation and governance are distinct: generated items must be validated and reviewed before they are treated as approved content. Second, duplicate detection and semantic similarity solve related but different problems, so both may be necessary in a practical assessment workflow. Third, a paper generator must distinguish hard eligibility conditions from soft preference targets and communicate infeasibility instead of implying exact compliance. Finally, question usage, organization scope, and output formatting must remain connected to the paper-building process.

AutoQgen is positioned as an implemented system-level combination of these concepts. It provides a taxonomy-linked question bank with authoring and import capabilities, AI-assisted candidate generation followed by draft import and review, duplicate checks, within-paper semantic similarity review, manual and rule-driven paper preparation, admission-based allocation, previous-use controls, reusable templates, and design-aware export. Its global and organization-level access-control model supports separate platform and institutional scopes. This positioning concerns workflow integration rather than the claim that AutoQgen invented the underlying methods or outperforms all prior systems.

\section{Summary}

This chapter reviewed digital question banks, AQG methods, review workflows, test assembly, access control, and AI/web security in the context of educational assessment systems. The literature shows that the major components of such systems have distinct but related roles: repositories provide structure, generation produces candidates, review judges suitability, similarity analysis identifies overlap, and paper assembly selects items under constraints. None of these should be treated as a complete substitute for the others.

AutoQgen is positioned as a system that integrates these components into one workflow: question management, AI-assisted draft creation, controlled review, organization-aware access, usage-aware paper generation, and export-ready assessment preparation. The next chapter builds on this foundation by presenting the system requirements and design of the implemented application.
